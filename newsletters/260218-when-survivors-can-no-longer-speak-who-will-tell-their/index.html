When Survivors Can No Longer Speak, Who Will Tell Their Stories?

← Back to Newsletters February 18, 2026

From Masha Pearl. Originally sent to The Blue Card email community on February 18, 2026.

Georgi Goldman introduces an AI-powered version of Holocaust survivor Sonia Warshawski, designed for student interaction.

Dear Friends,

At a Brooklyn synagogue recently, a group of Hebrew school students sat in front of a screen and began asking questions to Sonia Warshawski, a 100-year-old Holocaust survivor who endured three concentration camps, watched the Nazis march her mother to her death, and was shot in the chest on the day she was liberated from Bergen Belsen.

Sonia was not physically in the room. She was speaking through an AI-powered interactive avatar built from hundreds of recorded answers she gave about her life. The students asked about the death marches, about her family, about how she survived. Nearly every hand in the room went up. They leaned forward. They did not want it to end.

You can read more about this remarkable initiative in The Forward here

At The Blue Card, we helped bring this technology to twenty schools and community centers across the New York area this past year, with plans to expand nationwide. We did it because we understand what is ahead.

As June Koutros, principal of Temple Sholom Hebrew School, shared after her students participated: “We are profoundly grateful for the opportunity our students had to participate in the Blue Card Holocaust education program. The presentation was engaging and deeply meaningful, leaving a lasting impression on our Hebrew School students as well as the students who participated from St. Bernard. The program fostered thoughtful dialogue, compassion, and a deeper understanding of the importance of remembrance and standing against injustice.”

We were also moved by the response from St. Bernard Catholic Academy. Their principal shared how proud she was of her students and how meaningful the experience was for them, and is already working with her Social Studies teachers to plan a visit to bring the program directly to their Catholic school community.

Today, there are roughly 200,000 Holocaust survivors worldwide. In the next 15 years, 90% of them are projected to be gone. The most powerful model of Holocaust education, direct testimony from a living witness, is slipping away.

This technology is the closest we can come to preserving that living conversation. But when we asked survivors what they thought, we did not hear fear about artificial intelligence or hesitation about innovation. We heard something much more human and much more urgent.

Who will tell my story when I am no longer here?

That question stays with me.

Because the survivors we serve are not only thinking about memory. They are thinking about rent. Medication. Food. Heat in the winter. At The Blue Card, we work with these men and women every single day, not as symbols of history, but as people who need practical help and steady support.

We have a responsibility to preserve their stories. We also have a responsibility to care for them while they are still here to tell those stories.

I hope you will consider making a gift to our Holocaust Survivor Assistance Program today by clicking here. Every dollar goes directly to emergency financial aid, food assistance, and essential support that convey a powerful, simple message to a survivor: you are not forgotten. You matter. We are here.

Those students in Brooklyn showed us what it looks like to lean in and listen. Let us honor the survivors in the same way, not only by remembering, but by acting.
